Comeback Strength

The science behind your plan

Updated October 27, 2026.

Comeback Strength builds every plan from training principles that are well supported by sports science research and professional guidelines. This page explains the main ones and links to the studies. Numbers in brackets, like [1], point to the reference list at the bottom.

Two things to keep in mind. The studies below looked at groups of athletes doing a type of training, not at this app. And Comeback Strength is a training app, not medical care: it doesn't diagnose or treat injuries, and your doctor or physical therapist has the final say.

Coming back is a progression, not a single day

An international consensus of sports physical therapists describes return to sport as a continuum that runs alongside recovery, rather than one yes-or-no decision at the end [1]. That's why your plan moves through phases: Re-entry, Build, Power, then Return to Play, with your sport practice stepping up alongside the lifting.

Strength training first

A review of 25 randomized trials with over 26,000 athletes found that strength training programs cut sports injuries to about a third, while stretching programs showed no protective effect [2]. Balance training also helped. So the app is built around getting strong, with balance and landing work, rather than stretching routines.

How heavy, how many, how long to rest

  • Reps and weight follow the American College of Sports Medicine's guidelines: moderate weights for 8–12 reps while you find your feet, then heavier work in the 4–6 rep range for strength, and light, fast reps for power [3].
  • "Finish 1–2 reps short of failure" uses the reps-in-reserve scale, a simple way to judge effort that tracks well with how fast the bar moves [4].
  • When you beat the top of your rep range by 1–2 reps, the app tells you to add a little weight. That's the ACSM progression rule [3].
  • The 4- and 5-day plans work each main muscle group with several hard sets across the week, because more weekly sets (up to 10 or more per muscle) were linked to more muscle growth in a meta-analysis [5].
  • Rest is 2–3 minutes on main lifts and power work. Longer rest between sets led to more strength and muscle than 1 minute in trained lifters [6], and ACSM recommends 3–5 minutes for power training [3].
  • Every sixth week is a lighter deload week to shed fatigue, in line with an international expert consensus on deloading [7].

Power for your sport

Jumps and throws are part of every week because plyometric training reliably improves jump height, by roughly 5–9% on average [8]. For golf, strength and conditioning programs improved clubhead speed, ball speed and distance by about 4–6% on average across studies [9]. For throwing, a review found strength training generally raises throwing velocity, though the results vary by method and no single method stands out [10].

Exercises chosen for their injury research

  • Nordic hamstring curls: programs that included them had about half as many hamstring injuries across 15 studies and 8,459 athletes [11].
  • Copenhagen adductor exercise: a simple one-exercise program lowered the risk of groin problems by 41% in a trial with male soccer players [12]. The app uses the same exercise, the same three levels, and the study's rule of dropping a level if groin pain goes above 3 out of 10.
  • Jumping, landing, balance and strength work: programs that combine them were linked to about 50% fewer ACL injuries overall and about 67% fewer non-contact ACL injuries in female athletes [13].
  • Single-leg balance training: an 8-week home program cut repeat ankle sprains by about 37% [14], and a large review supports exercise therapy for preventing ankle problems from coming back [15].
  • A structured warm-up before every session: a soccer warm-up program (the FIFA 11+) led to about a third fewer injuries and nearly half as many severe injuries in young female players [16]. The app's warm-up isn't the 11+, but follows the same idea, and the app points soccer players to the 11+ itself.

Throwing and racquet athletes

In professional pitchers, weaker shoulder external rotation and supraspinatus strength before the season was linked to throwing injuries that needed surgery [17]. That's why throwers get shoulder rotation and shoulder-blade work every week. The specific arm-care exercises come from widely used programs like the Thrower's Ten and are chosen based on muscle-activation studies, not head-to-head injury trials [18].

The throwing progression follows the classic interval throwing program: start at 45 ft, build out to 180 ft, throw every other day, and only move on when a session was pain-free with no soreness the next day [19]. The same paper's interval programs for tennis and golf shaped those sports' progressions too.

Knees after ACL surgery

In a study of athletes after ACL reconstruction, those who returned to cutting and jumping sports before 9 months had about twice the rate of re-injury (39.5% vs 19.4%), and thigh strength under 90% of the other leg was linked to more re-injuries (33% vs 13%) [20]. If you tell the app when you had ACL surgery, it holds back cutting and big jumps, and keeps your sport practice at the Build level, until 9 months after. It can't measure strength, so it also reminds you to have your physical therapist test it before cutting sports.

Tendons and backs

  • Painful tendons respond well to gradually heavier, slow strength work. Heavy slow resistance training gave lasting improvement for patellar tendon pain, while steroid injections helped only in the short term [21]. And Achilles tendon patients who kept running and jumping with a pain-monitoring approach did as well as those who stopped [22].
  • For ongoing low back pain, exercise reduces pain compared with no treatment, but no single type of exercise has been shown to be best [23]. The app's early back choices (bracing, McGill-style core work, lighter barbell lifts at first) are a cautious approach, not a proven one.

Running

In new runners, those who raised their weekly distance by more than 30% tended to have more overuse injuries than those who kept increases under 10%, though the result fell just short of statistical certainty [24]. The app keeps increases small and steady.

What the research doesn't tell us

Many of these studies looked at specific groups, like male soccer players or professional pitchers, and results may differ for you. Some parts of the app, including the shoulder, hip and back modules, rest on expert consensus and muscle-activation research rather than injury trials. And the app itself hasn't been studied. We'll update this page as the research changes.
